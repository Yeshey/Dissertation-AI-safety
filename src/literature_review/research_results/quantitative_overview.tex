    \subsection{Quantitative Overview of the Field}\label{subsec:results_quantitative}
    % -----------------------------------------------------------------------

    The distribution of the selected literature across the seven concept-matrix categories is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:distribution_topics}. Raw article counts alone conflate two different questions: how much a topic is written about, and how dangerous a topic is. A category can dominate the literature because it is easy to publish on, not because it carries the greatest risk. To separate these two questions, we assign each category a \emph{severity score} and compare its rank against its \emph{coverage rank} (article count).

    \subsubsection{Severity Scoring Method}

    Each category is scored on four dimensions, adapted from established incident- and risk-severity frameworks (CVSS, FMEA), on a 1--5 ordinal scale:

    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Magnitude (M)}: ceiling of harm if the failure mode materializes (e.g.\ the Treacherous Turn, \cite{bostrom2014superintelligence}).
        \item \textbf{Certainty (C)}: extent of current empirical evidence, as opposed to purely theoretical risk.
        \item \textbf{Irreversibility (I)}: whether the harm is correctable after the fact.
        \item \textbf{Scope (S)}: breadth of population or systems exposed.
    \end{itemize}

    A single scorer produces an unfalsifiable, unreplicable number. To avoid this, five independent raters (domain experts / supervisors) score each category on all four dimensions. The full matrix of raw scores provided by all five raters is available in the Appendix (Table~\ref{tab:rater_scores}). Inter-rater agreement is reported via Krippendorff's $\alpha$; low agreement on a given dimension is itself a finding (it signals contested risk perception, not measurement error), and is reported alongside the score rather than hidden by averaging.
    
    \subsubsection{Scope Definition for Raters}

    Before scoring, all raters were given the following written scope, to ensure severity judgments reference a common referent rather than each rater's private notion of ``AI'':
        
    \begin{quote}
    \textbf{Scope of ``AI'' for this severity assessment.} Assess the potential harm of each category by considering both:

    \begin{enumerate}
        \item \textbf{Deployed and near-term systems}: current and near-future AI systems (roughly the next 0--3 years), including LLMs and other machine learning systems already in use. Base the assessment on documented incidents or failures that are reasonably likely given existing evidence and literature.

        \item \textbf{Frontier and projected systems}: AI systems that could plausibly emerge through continued improvements to current approaches (roughly a 3 to 15 year horizon, including AGI-adjacent capabilities). Base the assessment on technically grounded failure modes discussed in the research literature, such as deceptive alignment, mesa-optimization, and treacherous turns.
    \end{enumerate}

    Do not consider purely speculative or fictional scenarios that lack support in the current technical literature, such as generic science-fiction robot uprisings. When a category applies to both near-term and frontier systems (as is often the case for \emph{Alignment}), consider both perspectives when assigning its severity.
    \end{quote}

    Inter-rater reliability (IRR) was evaluated by calculating ordinal Krippendorff's $\alpha$ for each severity dimension across the seven categories. As presented in the final row of Table~\ref{tab:rater_scores_summary}, agreement across all four dimensions was near zero, falling well below the standard $\sim$0.67 threshold for acceptable agreement. This indicates that the five raters had virtually no consensus on how to rank the categories along these dimensions; the negative $\alpha$ for Certainty ($-0.113$) even suggests systematic disagreement. \textcolor{red}{Given this lack of consensus, category severity numbers below should be treated as provisional pending rater re-discussion — low $N$ (5 raters) and no calibration round likely both contribute. Flagging rather than silently averaging, per stated methodology.}

    Per-category severity is computed two ways, checked against each other for robustness, mirroring the ordinal-vs-cardinal validation logic in \cite{vei2025aiharmonics}:

    \begin{enumerate}

        \item \textbf{Weighted score} (cardinal, assumes equal-interval scale): mean each dimension across raters, then
        \[
        \text{Severity}_i = 0.4 \cdot \bar{M}_i + 0.3 \cdot \bar{C}_i + 0.2 \cdot \bar{I}_i + 0.1 \cdot \bar{S}_i
        \]
        with weights favoring harm magnitude over current evidence, consistent with a precautionary approach to existential-risk-adjacent research \cite{amodei2016concrete}.
        \item \textbf{Rank-based score} (ordinal, no interval assumption): convert each dimension to a rank across the seven categories, combine via Borda count. This avoids assuming that the distance between a score of 2 and 3 equals the distance between 4 and 5 — an assumption the weighted score silently makes, and one recent ordinal-risk work explicitly avoids \cite{vei2025aiharmonics, giudici2023cyberrisk}.
    \end{enumerate}

    Spearman correlation between the two methods: $\rho = 0.964$ ($n=7$) — orderings agree except one adjacent swap at ranks 6/7 (Liability \& Ethics / Explainability). Per the stated rule, this counts as close enough agreement to report the weighted score as primary, with the ordinal method as robustness check, noting the last-place tie is not fully resolved between methods.

    \subsubsection{Severity vs.\ Coverage: the Gap Index}

    For each category $i$, define
    \[
    \text{GapIndex}_i = \text{CoverageRank}_i - \text{SeverityRank}_i
    \]
    where both ranks run 1 (most severe / most covered) to 7 (least). A positive GapIndex means the category is riskier than its literature volume reflects — understudied relative to danger. A negative GapIndex means the field has invested more attention than the severity score alone would justify.

    Spearman rank correlation between SeverityRank and CoverageRank: $\rho = 0.857$ — attention tracks danger reasonably well, no wholesale systemic mismatch. \emph{Human \& Societal Impacts} and \emph{Auditing, Safety \& Oversight} are perfectly matched (gap $0$). \emph{Governance}, \emph{Bias \& Fairness}, and \emph{Liability \& Ethics} form a tied cluster at gap $+1$ — each modestly understudied relative to danger, none standing out sharply from the others. The clearest single signal in the data is the other direction: \emph{Explainability / Interpretability} carries the largest-magnitude gap ($-2$) — least severe category by both scoring methods, yet mid-pack in coverage (12 articles, rank 5) — relatively \emph{over}-studied given its risk profile. \emph{Alignment} shows a smaller negative gap ($-1$): second-most-covered category (29 articles) but only third by severity, so already well-attended relative to danger rather than neglected.

    This reframes the original claim: rather than "the most dangerous category is understudied," the evidence supports a narrower statement — overall attention allocation roughly tracks severity ($\rho=0.857$), Explainability/Interpretability stands out as relatively over-researched given its (currently lowest-ranked) severity, and Governance, Bias \& Fairness, and Liability \& Ethics show a shared, modest under-study signal worth flagging rather than one dramatic omission.

    \subsubsection{Existential-Risk Specificity}

    One further methodological gap: the category label ``Alignment'' is not synonymous with ``existential risk.'' Many Alignment papers address narrow technical alignment (e.g.\ RLHF evaluation, reward hacking in bounded settings) without engaging catastrophic or existential failure modes (deceptive alignment, mesa-optimization, treacherous turn). Reporting category-level severity as a proxy for x-risk coverage overstates the claim. To support an existential-risk-specific argument, each of the $N=29$ Alignment articles (and any others touching catastrophic risk, e.g.\ in Auditing) should be tagged as \texttt{x-risk-relevant} or \texttt{narrow-technical} during full-text review, and the x-risk subset's count reported separately from the category total. \textcolor{red}{[PLACEHOLDER — tagging not yet performed; this is unrelated to the severity-rater dataset above and still needs its own pass.]}

    \subsubsection{Sensitivity Analysis}

    Two robustness checks, following the boundary- and permutation-analysis logic of \cite{vei2025aiharmonics}:

    \begin{enumerate}
        \item \textbf{Weight perturbation}: Severity$_i$ recomputed under 20 randomly drawn weight vectors (Dirichlet, summing to 1, constrained so magnitude stays the highest-weighted dimension). Spearman correlation between the base ranking and each perturbed ranking: $\rho_{\min} = 0.964$, $\rho_{\text{mean}} = 0.986$, $\rho_{\max} = 1.000$. Ranking stable — not an artifact of the 0.4/0.3/0.2/0.1 choice.
        \item \textbf{Rater-dropout}: Severity$_i$ recomputed leaving out one rater at a time (5 leave-one-out scenarios, since $N=5$ raters now, not 3). \emph{Human \& Societal Impacts} takes rank 1 in 4/5 scenarios (drops to rank 2, behind Alignment, only when Rater 3 is excluded — Rater 3 gave Alignment a 4/2/4/4 profile that pulls the mean down less than others). \emph{Explainability / Interpretability} stays last or second-to-last in all 5/5 scenarios, so its negative Gap Index is the most rater-dropout-robust finding in this table. The middle cluster (Governance, Auditing, Bias, Liability) reshuffles order somewhat across drops — consistent with the near-zero Krippendorff's $\alpha$ above, i.e.\ the finding is more fragile there than the top/bottom of the ranking.
    \end{enumerate}

    If rankings hold up under both checks, the Gap Index result can be reported with reasonable confidence; if not, only directionally (e.g.\ "several categories show positive gaps, most consistently Liability \& Ethics").
