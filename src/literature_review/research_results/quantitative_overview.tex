    \subsection{Quantitative Overview of the Field}\label{subsec:results_quantitative}
    % -----------------------------------------------------------------------

    The distribution of the selected literature across the seven concept-matrix categories is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:distribution_topics}. Raw article counts alone conflate two different questions: how much a topic is written about, and how dangerous a topic is. A category can dominate the literature because it is easy to publish on, not because it carries the greatest risk. To separate these two questions, we assign each category a \emph{severity score} and compare its rank against its \emph{coverage rank} (article count).

    \subsubsection{Severity Scoring Method}

    Each category is scored on four dimensions, adapted from established incident- and risk-severity frameworks (CVSS, FMEA), on a 1--5 ordinal scale:

    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Magnitude (M)}: ceiling of harm if the failure mode materializes (e.g.\ the Treacherous Turn, \cite{bostrom2014superintelligence}).
        \item \textbf{Certainty (C)}: extent of current empirical evidence, as opposed to purely theoretical risk.
        \item \textbf{Irreversibility (I)}: whether the harm is correctable after the fact.
        \item \textbf{Scope (S)}: breadth of population or systems exposed.
    \end{itemize}

    A single scorer produces an unfalsifiable, unreplicable number. To avoid this, three independent raters (domain experts / supervisors) score each category on all four dimensions. Inter-rater agreement is reported via Krippendorff's $\alpha$; low agreement on a given category is itself a finding (it signals contested risk perception, not measurement error), and is reported alongside the score rather than hidden by averaging.

    \subsubsection{Scope Definition for Raters}

    Before scoring, all raters were given the following written scope, to ensure severity judgments reference a common referent rather than each rater's private notion of ``AI'':
        
    \begin{quote}
    \textbf{Scope of ``AI'' for this severity assessment.} Assess the potential harm of each category by considering both:

    \begin{enumerate}
        \item \textbf{Deployed and near-term systems}: current and near-future AI systems (roughly the next 0--3 years), including LLMs and other machine learning systems already in use. Base the assessment on documented incidents or failures that are reasonably likely given existing evidence and literature.

        \item \textbf{Frontier and projected systems}: AI systems that could plausibly emerge through continued improvements to current approaches (roughly a 3 to 15 year horizon, including AGI-adjacent capabilities). Base the assessment on technically grounded failure modes discussed in the research literature, such as deceptive alignment, mesa-optimization, and treacherous turns.
    \end{enumerate}

    Do not consider purely speculative or fictional scenarios that lack support in the current technical literature, such as generic science-fiction robot uprisings. When a category applies to both near-term and frontier systems (as is often the case for \emph{Alignment}), consider both perspectives when assigning its severity.
    \end{quote}

    Krippendorff's $\alpha$ across the three raters: \textcolor{red}{[PLACEHOLDER, e.g. $\alpha = 0.81$]}. Values above $\sim$0.67 are generally treated as acceptable agreement \cite{robinson2024likert}; categories falling below this threshold should be flagged and re-discussed among raters rather than silently averaged.

    Per-category severity is computed two ways, and the two are checked against each other for robustness, mirroring the ordinal-vs-cardinal validation logic in \cite{vei2025aiharmonics}:

    \begin{enumerate}
        \item \textbf{Weighted score} (cardinal, assumes equal-interval scale): mean each dimension across raters, then
        \[
        \text{Severity}_i = 0.4 \cdot \bar{M}_i + 0.3 \cdot \bar{C}_i + 0.2 \cdot \bar{I}_i + 0.1 \cdot \bar{S}_i
        \]
        with weights favoring harm magnitude over current evidence, consistent with a precautionary approach to existential-risk-adjacent research \cite{amodei2016concrete}.
        \item \textbf{Rank-based score} (ordinal, no interval assumption): convert each dimension to a rank across the seven categories, combine via Borda count. This avoids assuming that the distance between a score of 2 and 3 equals the distance between 4 and 5 — an assumption the weighted score silently makes, and one recent ordinal-risk work explicitly avoids \cite{vei2025aiharmonics, giudici2023cyberrisk}.
    \end{enumerate}

    If the two methods produce the same category ordering (checked via Spearman correlation between them), the weighted score can be reported as the primary measure since it is more interpretable, with the ordinal method serving as a robustness check. If they diverge, the ordinal ranking is preferred, since it makes fewer assumptions.

    \subsubsection{Severity vs.\ Coverage: the Gap Index}

    For each category $i$, define
    \[
    \text{GapIndex}_i = \text{CoverageRank}_i - \text{SeverityRank}_i
    \]
    where both ranks run 1 (most severe / most covered) to 7 (least). A positive GapIndex means the category is riskier than its literature volume reflects — i.e.\ understudied relative to danger. A negative GapIndex means the opposite — the field has invested more attention than the severity score alone would justify.

    We additionally report the Spearman rank correlation $\rho$ between SeverityRank and CoverageRank across all seven categories. A high $\rho$ ($\to 1$) indicates the field is already broadly allocating attention in proportion to danger; a low or negative $\rho$ indicates a systemic mismatch. This single number replaces impressionistic claims like "X is under-studied" with something falsifiable.

    With these placeholder values, $\rho \approx 0.82$: severity and coverage track each other reasonably well overall — this dataset does not support a claim of a wholesale, systemic mismatch between danger and research volume. The picture is more targeted than the earlier draft suggested. \emph{Alignment}, once correctly identified as the \emph{second}-most-covered category (29 articles, behind Human \& Societal Impacts' 35), is not dramatically neglected — it already receives substantial attention proportional to its top severity ranking, though a modest positive gap remains. The clearest positive gap in this illustrative data is \emph{Liability \& Ethics}: a mid-tier severity score (rank 5) paired with the lowest coverage (tied last, 8 articles) — a modest-danger category that is nonetheless barely studied. \emph{Auditing, Safety \& Oversight} shows a similar, smaller pattern. Categories with negative Gap Index (\emph{Human \& Societal Impacts}, \emph{Governance}, \emph{Explainability}, \emph{Bias \& Fairness}) are, if anything, over-represented relative to their severity scores.

    This reframes the original claim: rather than "the most dangerous category is understudied," the evidence (once corrected) supports a narrower statement — the field's overall allocation of attention roughly tracks severity, with \emph{Liability \& Ethics} standing out as the category most disproportionately under-researched given its risk profile, and Alignment/Auditing showing smaller residual gaps worth flagging rather than a dramatic omission.

    \subsubsection{Existential-Risk Specificity}

    One further methodological gap: the category label ``Alignment'' is not synonymous with ``existential risk.'' Many Alignment papers address narrow technical alignment (e.g.\ RLHF evaluation, reward hacking in bounded settings) without engaging catastrophic or existential failure modes (deceptive alignment, mesa-optimization, treacherous turn). Reporting category-level severity as a proxy for x-risk coverage overstates the claim. To support an existential-risk-specific argument, each of the $N=29$ Alignment articles (and any others touching catastrophic risk, e.g.\ in Auditing) should be tagged as \texttt{x-risk-relevant} or \texttt{narrow-technical} during full-text review, and the x-risk subset's count reported separately from the category total. \textcolor{red}{[PLACEHOLDER — tagging not yet performed; e.g.\ suppose 11/29 Alignment articles are x-risk-relevant — that subset, not the full 29, is the correct denominator for any existential-risk-specific claim.]}

    \subsubsection{Sensitivity Analysis}

    To confirm the Gap Index ranking is not an artifact of the chosen weights (0.4/0.3/0.2/0.1) or of any single rater's judgment, two robustness checks are run, following the boundary- and permutation-analysis logic of \cite{vei2025aiharmonics}:

    \begin{enumerate}
        \item \textbf{Weight perturbation}: recompute Severity$_i$ under $\sim$20 randomly perturbed weight vectors (still summing to 1, magnitude weighted highest). Report the Spearman correlation between the original category ranking and each perturbed ranking. \textcolor{red}{[PLACEHOLDER: e.g.\ $\rho \geq 0.9$ across all perturbations $\Rightarrow$ ranking stable.]}
        \item \textbf{Rater-dropout}: recompute Severity$_i$ leaving out one rater at a time (3 leave-one-out scenarios). Report how much the category ranking shifts. \textcolor{red}{[PLACEHOLDER: e.g.\ Liability \& Ethics stays top-gap category in 3/3 leave-one-out scenarios $\Rightarrow$ finding not driven by a single rater.]}
    \end{enumerate}

    If rankings hold up under both checks, the Gap Index result can be reported with reasonable confidence; if not, only directionally (e.g.\ "several categories show positive gaps, most consistently Liability \& Ethics").
