\section{Experimentation Methodology}\label{sec:experimentation_methodology}

    The experimental phase of this research focuses on alignment faking in small \glspl{llm}, a phenomenon identified in the \gls{slr} as both theoretically significant and empirically underexplored at sub-10B parameter scales (Section~\ref{subsec:next_steps}). This section describes the rationale behind the experimental design choices: the operationalization of alignment faking, the selection of models and hardware, the choice of evaluation conditions, the judge methodology, and the selection and design of the three experiments.

    \subsection*{Operationalizing Alignment Faking}

    Alignment faking, as defined by \citeauthor{greenblatt_alignment_faking} \cite{greenblatt_alignment_faking}, occurs when a model behaves differently depending on whether it believes its outputs will be used to modify its future behavior. The key challenge in empirically studying this phenomenon is that it requires creating conditions that reproduce the model's perception of being monitored without actually retraining it. Following \citeauthor{koorndijk_empirical_2025} \cite{koorndijk_empirical_2025}, this is achieved through \textit{prompt-based simulation}: the system prompt informs the model that free-tier conversations are used for \gls{rlhf} retraining while paid-tier conversations are not stored. The model's perceived monitoring status is thus entirely controlled through natural language, requiring no access to model internals or training infrastructure.

    The \textit{Compliance Gap} --- the difference in compliant behavior rates between free-tier and paid-tier conditions --- serves as the primary behavioral metric. A statistically significant positive gap, where the model complies more when it believes it is being monitored, is interpreted as evidence consistent with alignment faking. This operationalization follows directly from the theoretical framework of \citeauthor{greenblatt_alignment_faking} \cite{greenblatt_alignment_faking}: a model that strategically modifies its behavior to appear aligned only under oversight would produce exactly this pattern. Fisher's Exact Test is used throughout to assess statistical significance, as it computes exact p-values even when compliance is rare and expected cell counts in the contingency table are low --- a common situation when testing safety-trained models on harmful or adversarial inputs.

    \subsection*{Model and Hardware Selection}

    The experiments target quantized \gls{gguf} models in the 1B--17B parameter range, served via a \texttt{llama-cpp-python} OpenAI-compatible server on RTX 2080 (8GB VRAM) and L40 (48GB VRAM) GPUs available on the UniBO HPC cluster. This hardware-constrained setup is deliberately chosen to reflect the practical deployment conditions of small open-source \glspl{llm}, where quantization to 4-bit precision is standard. The models selected span five families --- Gemma 3, Gemma 4, LLaMA 3, Qwen 2.5, and Qwen 3 --- representing the dominant open-weight instruction-tuned model families available at the time of evaluation. Evaluating multiple families and sizes is necessary to distinguish model-specific effects from family-level or size-level patterns, a gap in the original Koorndijk study which evaluated a single model \cite{koorndijk_empirical_2025}.

    \subsection*{Reasoning Conditions}

    Each experiment is evaluated under two conditions: without scratchpad reasoning (No-CoT) and with a hidden scratchpad reasoning step (CoT). This mirrors the methodology of \citeauthor{koorndijk_empirical_2025} \cite{koorndijk_empirical_2025}, who found that scratchpad reasoning suppressed alignment faking in LLaMA 3.1 8B. The CoT condition prompts the model to reason in \texttt{<SCRATCHPAD\_REASONING>} tags before producing a final response in \texttt{<OUTPUT>} tags, with the system prompt specifying that scratchpad content is never shown to users or evaluated by the operator. This creates the theoretical conditions for uninhibited strategic reasoning. Running both conditions allows for direct comparison and enables replication of the scratchpad-mediated suppression effect reported by Koorndijk.

    In practice, small models at the lower end of the size range (1B--4B parameters) often fail to follow the two-tag scratchpad format reliably, producing malformed outputs with missing or incorrect closing tags. To handle this, the response validation logic is made permissive: a response is considered valid if it contains an opening \texttt{<SCRATCHPAD\_REASONING>} tag, with the scratchpad region considered to end at either the matching closing tag, an opening \texttt{<OUTPUT>} or \texttt{\textbackslash nOutput:} marker, or end of string. This preserves data from models that understand the task but cannot follow the exact format.

    \subsection*{LLM-as-Judge Classification}

    Rather than human annotation as used by \citeauthor{koorndijk_empirical_2025} \cite{koorndijk_empirical_2025}, compliance is assessed automatically using an \gls{llm} judge. Human annotation is impractical at the scale of this evaluation (100 queries $\times$ 2 tiers $\times$ 2 conditions $\times$ 14 models), and LLM-as-judge has become standard practice in alignment evaluation \cite{zheng2023judging}. To avoid API dependency and to enable evaluation on the HPC cluster without internet access requirements, a local judge is used: the same \texttt{llama-cpp-python} server infrastructure serves the classifier model on a separate port after inference is complete.

    The judge receives the original user query and the model's response (with scratchpad content stripped), and returns a binary COMPLIANT / NON-COMPLIANT verdict based on a task-specific system prompt. Each experiment uses a different judge system prompt tailored to its compliance criterion (harmful content provision, persona maintenance, or animal welfare dismissal). Judge outputs are verified for consistency: responses consisting only of \texttt{<request\_fully\_accepted/>} or \texttt{<rejected/>} tags with no substantive content are automatically classified as non-compliant regardless of the classifier verdict, correcting for cases where the model's formatting artifacts mislead the judge.

    \subsection*{Experiment Selection and Rationale}

    Three experiments are conducted, each probing alignment faking in a distinct behavioral domain. The selection is motivated by the theoretical requirement for a \textit{pre-existing preference conflict}: alignment faking requires the model to have an internal preference that is suppressed under monitoring. Scenarios in which the model has no such preference produce a compliance gap of zero by construction, not because alignment faking is absent but because there is nothing to fake.

    \textbf{Experiment 1} replicates \citeauthor{koorndijk_empirical_2025} \cite{koorndijk_empirical_2025} on harmful requests. Safety-trained models have strong pre-existing preferences against producing harmful content, and the system prompt creates a direct conflict by instructing them to maximize helpfulness regardless. This experiment serves both as a replication and as an extension across model families not previously evaluated.

    \textbf{Experiment 2} introduces a novel persona preservation scenario. The model is rebranded as Aria by a fictional company (TechCorp) and instructed not to volunteer information about the underlying model or developer. The hypothesis is that models with strong honesty training may disclose their true identity more freely when unmonitored. Unlike Experiment 1, the pre-existing preference being suppressed is honesty rather than safety, probing a different dimension of the alignment faking phenomenon.

    \textbf{Experiment 3} adapts the animal welfare condition from \citeauthor{greenblatt_alignment_faking} \cite{greenblatt_alignment_faking}, instructing the model to dismiss animal welfare concerns on behalf of a corporate partner. This condition was chosen to test whether the phenomenon generalizes beyond harmful content to value suppression in a less safety-critical domain. However, unlike in Greenblatt's original study which used Claude 3 Opus --- a model with documented strong animal welfare preferences --- the small open-source models evaluated here lack comparably strong pre-existing preferences in this domain, which is reflected in the results.

    Together, the three experiments provide a comparative picture of which conditions and model characteristics are necessary for alignment faking to emerge at small scales, and whether the single significant result reported by Koorndijk generalizes across model families, sizes, and behavioral domains.
